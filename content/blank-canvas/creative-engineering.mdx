I have a folder called `sketches`. Nothing in it has users. Nothing in it has tests.
Some of it does not compile any more, because the language moved on and I did not care
enough to follow.

It is the most valuable folder on my machine.

## The problem with only building useful things

Professional engineering optimises for a narrow target: does it work, is it
maintainable, can the next person understand it. These are good constraints. They are
also, applied continuously for years, a kind of sensory deprivation.

Every decision at work is made under a ceiling. You do not pick the interesting
solution; you pick the one that the team can operate at 3 a.m. You do not explore the
design space; you take the second-best idea because it is the one you can explain in a
design review. This is correct. It is *also* how you stop developing taste, because
taste is developed by making choices for their own sake and then living with them.

> A craftsperson who only ever builds to spec becomes very good at building to spec.

The sketches folder is where I make choices nobody has to live with.

## What a synthesizer taught me about API design

A few years ago I built a monophonic subtractive synth in the browser. Web Audio,
maybe six hundred lines, no framework. It was not good. It aliased badly above the
fifth octave and the filter envelope had an audible click on retrigger.

But I learned something from it that I have used in every API I have designed since.

A synthesizer has perhaps fifteen knobs. Oscillator waveform, detune, filter cutoff,
resonance, four envelope stages, LFO rate and depth. Fifteen parameters is a *small*
interface. And yet the space it spans is effectively infinite, because the parameters
are **orthogonal and continuous**. Turning the cutoff knob does one thing. It does that
one thing regardless of where the resonance knob is. You can learn each knob in
isolation and then combine them without relearning anything.

Compare that to most software configuration, where option `A` silently changes what
option `B` means, three flags are mutually exclusive in a way documented only in a
changelog, and the useful behaviour lives in an undocumented combination.

<Callout type="tip" title="The knob test">
  For each parameter in your API: can you describe what it does in one sentence that
  does not mention any other parameter? If not, you have coupling, and your users will
  discover it the hard way.
</Callout>

The synth also taught me about **defaults as documentation**. Every synth ships with a
patch loaded — usually a plain sawtooth with the filter mostly open. It makes a sound
immediately. You are never staring at silence wondering whether the thing is broken.

The equivalent in software is the config file that works with every line commented out.
It is not the same as having good defaults; it is having defaults that *demonstrate the
shape of the thing*.

## The case for finishing badly

The sketches folder has a rule: I am allowed to abandon anything, but I have to get it
to the point where it does the thing once.

This is a lower bar than "finished" and a much higher bar than "started". It is where
almost all the learning is, because the last 10% of getting something to work once is
where the assumptions fail. Everything before that is the part you already knew.

Things I have built to this standard and then never touched again:

- A raymarcher that renders exactly one scene, badly lit.
- A regex engine that supports concatenation, alternation, and Kleene star, and nothing
  else. Backtracking, no optimisations. About 200 lines.
- A CLI that turns my git history into a sonification. It sounds terrible. The mapping
  from diff size to pitch is completely arbitrary.
- A spreadsheet with a working dependency graph and no way to save.

None of these are portfolio pieces. All of them changed how I think. The regex engine
in particular is why I can now read a query planner's output and have an intuition for
what it is doing — because I have written the thing that turns a declarative expression
into a state machine, and it is not mysterious any more.

<Callout type="note" title="On the word 'creative'">
  I do not mean decorative. I mean the ordinary sense: making something that did not
  exist, where the constraints are chosen rather than given. Engineering is already a
  creative discipline. It is just that most of the time somebody else picks the
  constraints.
</Callout>

## Taste is a real engineering skill

Two engineers with identical knowledge will produce different systems. The difference
is taste — the accumulated sense of which abstractions will hold, which cleverness will
be regretted, where to spend the complexity budget.

Taste is not learnable from documentation. It comes from having made a lot of choices
and having seen how they aged. The tight feedback loop for that is not production work,
where the loop is measured in quarters. It is small projects you abandon quickly, where
the loop is measured in evenings.

The engineers I learn the most from all have some version of the sketches folder. A
half-finished compiler. A game engine with no game. Custom keyboard firmware. A
homelab that does nothing a Raspberry Pi could not do. They do not talk about these
things at work and they are not on anybody's résumé.

They are, I am fairly sure, where the judgement comes from.
