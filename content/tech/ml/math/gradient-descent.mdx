Gradient descent is one line of code and about a page of justification. The line is
easy to memorise and easy to misuse; the page is what tells you why your loss curve is
oscillating.

## The setup

We have a loss $L(\theta)$ over parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^n$, and we want to
find $\theta^\star = \arg\min_\theta L(\theta)$. We cannot solve $\nabla L = 0$ in
closed form, so we do the next best thing: start somewhere and repeatedly step downhill.

The gradient $\nabla L(\theta)$ is the vector of partial derivatives:

$$
\nabla L(\theta) = \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_1}, \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_2}, \ldots, \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_n} \right]^{\top}
$$

Its key property is directional: for a unit vector $u$, the rate of change of $L$ along
$u$ is $\nabla L(\theta)^{\top} u$. By Cauchy&ndash;Schwarz this is maximised when $u$
points along $\nabla L$, and minimised when it points along $-\nabla L$.

**So the steepest descent direction is $-\nabla L(\theta)$.** That is the whole idea.

## The update rule

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t)
$$

where $\eta > 0$ is the learning rate.

## Why it actually decreases the loss

Take the first-order Taylor expansion of $L$ around $\theta_t$:

$$
L(\theta_t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_t)) \approx L(\theta_t) - \eta \lVert \nabla L(\theta_t) \rVert^2
$$

The subtracted term is $\eta$ times a squared norm, so it is non-negative. For small
enough $\eta$, the step strictly decreases the loss unless the gradient is already zero.

That phrase — *for small enough* — is doing all the work, and it is exactly what breaks.

<Callout type="note" title="The approximation has a horizon">
  Taylor's theorem is local. The linear model of $L$ is only trustworthy in a
  neighbourhood of $\theta_t$. A large $\eta$ steps outside that neighbourhood, where
  the prediction "the loss goes down" carries no weight at all.
</Callout>

## Where it diverges

Suppose $L$ is $\beta$-smooth, meaning its gradient is Lipschitz with constant $\beta$:

$$
\lVert \nabla L(x) - \nabla L(y) \rVert \leq \beta \lVert x - y \rVert
$$

Smoothness gives an upper bound on the loss after a step:

$$
L(\theta_{t+1}) \leq L(\theta_t) - \eta\left(1 - \frac{\beta \eta}{2}\right) \lVert \nabla L(\theta_t) \rVert^2
$$

The bracketed factor is positive exactly when $\eta < 2/\beta$. So:

- $\eta < 1/\beta$ &mdash; steady monotone descent.
- $1/\beta < \eta < 2/\beta$ &mdash; still converges, but overshoots and oscillates
  across the valley.
- $\eta > 2/\beta$ &mdash; the guarantee is gone. The loss climbs, and it climbs
  geometrically.

The single most common cause of a training run that produces `NaN` is $\eta$ on the
wrong side of $2/\beta$.

## Concretely: least squares

For $L(\theta) = \frac{1}{2m} \lVert X\theta - y \rVert^2$, the gradient is

$$
\nabla L(\theta) = \frac{1}{m} X^{\top}(X\theta - y)
$$

and the smoothness constant is $\beta = \lambda_{\max}(X^{\top}X) / m$ &mdash; the
largest eigenvalue of the scaled Gram matrix. This is why **feature scaling matters**:
an unscaled feature inflates $\lambda_{\max}$, which shrinks the largest safe $\eta$,
which forces every other parameter to crawl.

```python title="gradient_descent.py" showLineNumbers {12-15}
import numpy as np

def gradient_descent(X, y, lr=0.01, epochs=1_000, tol=1e-8):
    """Batch gradient descent for least squares. Returns (theta, history)."""
    m, n = X.shape
    theta = np.zeros(n)
    history = []

    for epoch in range(epochs):
        residual = X @ theta - y
        loss = (residual @ residual) / (2 * m)

        grad = (X.T @ residual) / m          # shape (n,)
        step = lr * grad
        theta -= step

        history.append(loss)

        # Converged when the step stops moving us anywhere.
        if np.linalg.norm(step) < tol:
            break

    return theta, history
```

And the diagnostic worth keeping next to it:

```python title="lr_bound.py" showLineNumbers
def max_safe_lr(X):
    """Learning rates at or above this diverge for least squares."""
    m = X.shape[0]
    beta = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X).max() / m
    return 2.0 / beta
```

If your chosen learning rate is anywhere near `max_safe_lr(X)`, the oscillation you are
seeing is not noise. It is the bound.

<Callout type="tip" title="Reading a loss curve">
  Smooth and slow: raise $\eta$. Jagged but trending down: you are between
  $1/\beta$ and $2/\beta$ &mdash; usually fine, often the fastest setting. Rising or
  `NaN`: you are past $2/\beta$; cut $\eta$ by 10x rather than tweaking it.
</Callout>

## Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch

| Variant | Gradient per step | Cost per step | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Batch | All $m$ examples | $O(mn)$ | Exact direction, smooth, slow |
| Stochastic | 1 example | $O(n)$ | Noisy, escapes shallow minima |
| Mini-batch | $b$ examples | $O(bn)$ | The practical default |

Mini-batch wins for a reason that is not statistical: a batch of 32 to 512 is what
saturates a GPU's matrix units. The variance reduction is real, but the hardware
argument is the one that actually sets the number.

## What to remember

The update rule is a consequence, not a definition. It follows from taking the steepest
direction and trusting a linear approximation for a short distance. Every failure mode
of gradient descent &mdash; divergence, oscillation, glacial progress on ill-conditioned
data &mdash; is a statement about how far you can trust that approximation, which is to
say, about $\beta$.
